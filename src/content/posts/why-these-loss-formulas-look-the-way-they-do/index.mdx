You can skip it and go straight to [Part 7](/post/paper-1-part-7-learning-by-trial-and-error-gradient-descent/) (gradient descent) without missing anything you need. It's here because Part 6 took both loss formulas as given without saying where they come from.

Why the `max`? Why add `1`? Why raise `e` to a power instead of just taking an absolute value or squaring the score? Without those answers, the formulas can look like they were pulled out of a hat.

Each one is built backward from a goal. Every piece does a specific job. Here's what each piece is for.

## What any loss function has to do

Before any specific formula, a loss has to satisfy three things:

1. It's small (ideally zero) when the prediction is good.
2. It grows when the prediction is bad.
3. You can push it downward by nudging the weights.

A loss exists to be made *smaller* by the training process (that's what Part 7 covers). So the formula has to respond to weight changes in a way that points toward better weights.

Lots of formulas satisfy these three requirements. Hinge and softmax are two of those, but they aren't the only possible choices. They're just two well-worn options, each derived from a goal.

## Hinge loss, one symbol at a time

The goal behind hinge loss: **make the correct label's score beat the wrong label's score by a comfortable buffer.**

```
loss = max(0, 1 + wrong_score - correct_score)
```

Here's what each part does.

**Why a difference (`wrong_score - correct_score`) instead of the raw scores?** Because the absolute size of the scores is meaningless; only the gap between them matters. A classifier scoring cat = 1000, dog = 999 is barely better than a coin flip. One scoring cat = 5, dog = -5 is more confident. What separates them is the gap, so the loss depends on the gap, not the raw numbers.

**Why `wrong_score - correct_score` and not `correct_score - wrong_score`?** Because a loss has to get bigger when the prediction gets worse, and only one of the two orderings does that. `wrong_score - correct_score` is positive exactly when a wrong label beats the correct one, which is the bad case, so it works as a penalty: large and positive when things are bad, zero or negative when they're fine.

The reverse, `correct_score - wrong_score`, is large when things are good, since it measures how far the correct label is *ahead*. That quantity has a name, the margin, and it's the natural thing to plot on a graph, but it's the wrong sign to hand to a penalty. The formula flips it back anyway: `1 + wrong_score - correct_score` is the same as `1 - (correct_score - wrong_score)`, or `1 - margin`. The two orderings are just negatives of each other, and hinge loss uses the one that already grows in the bad direction.

Scenario C from Part 6 (a wrong guess, cat = 3, dog = 10) shows why the sign matters:

```
wrong_score - correct_score = 10 - 3 = +7   (positive: works as a penalty)
correct_score - wrong_score = 3 - 10 = -7   (negative: useless as a penalty)
```

**Why `max(0, ...)`?** This is the piece that gives hinge loss its name and its behavior. Suppose you dropped the `max` and just used `1 + wrong_score - correct_score`. Then a prediction that's already correct by a wide margin would produce a large negative loss, and the training process would keep trying to drive that margin even wider, spending effort on examples that are already fine. The `max(0, ...)` says: "once you clear the required margin, your loss is exactly zero." It caps the demand at good-enough. That threshold, past which an example is satisfied and contributes nothing, is what the `max` is for.

Scenario B from Part 6 (a confident, correct guess, cat = 100, dog = 1) shows what the `max` does:

```
without max:  1 + 1 - 100 = -98   (a big negative "reward" for being right)
with max:     max(0, -98)  = 0    (no reward, no penalty: nothing left to do)
```

**Why the `1`?** That's the size of the buffer. Without it, the loss would drop to zero the moment the correct score edged ahead of the wrong score by any amount at all, even 0.0001. That's fragile: a speck of noise flips the answer. The `1` demands the correct score win by a clear margin, not barely.

Scenario A from Part 6 (a close call, cat = 10, dog = 9.5) shows the difference:

```
without the 1:  max(0, 9.5 - 10)     = 0     (a hair ahead already counts as fine)
with the 1:     max(0, 1 + 9.5 - 10) = 0.5   (still penalized until the gap reaches 1)
```

**But why 1 specifically, and not 3 or 0.5?** Because the scores have no fixed scale. The classifier can multiply all its weights up or down, which scales all its scores up or down, so it can make a margin of "1" mean whatever it needs. Pick any positive number and you get an equivalent classifier with rescaled weights. The `1` is just a convention that pins down the scale. Its exact value doesn't matter, because the weights adjust around it.

So `max(0, 1 + wrong_score - correct_score)` reads, in full: "zero penalty if the correct score beats the wrong score by at least 1; otherwise the penalty grows in proportion to how far short you fell."

## Softmax loss, one symbol at a time

Different goal: **turn the scores into a probability for each label, then penalize the model by how little probability it assigned to the correct label.** The less probability on the correct label, the bigger the penalty.

```
probability_cat = exp(score_cat) / (exp(score_cat) + exp(score_dog) + exp(score_bird))

# cat is the correct label in this example, so the loss uses probability_cat
loss = -log(probability_cat)
```

**Why exponentiate (`exp(score)`)?** Scores can be any number, negative or positive, but probabilities can't be negative. `exp(x)` means the fixed number `e` (about 2.718, a constant the way π is about 3.14159) raised to the power `x`, and it turns any number into a positive one. (More on why `exp` specifically, and not some other way of forcing positivity, in the next section.)

**Why divide by the sum of all the `exp` values?** Dividing each positive number by their total forces the results to add up to 1, which is the defining property of a set of probabilities. That step just turns a handful of positive numbers into proportions.

**Why `-log(probability of the correct label)`?** Two reasons:

- The penalty rises as the correct answer's probability falls. When that probability is 1, that means `-log(1)` is 0, which means the penalty is zero. As the probability drops toward 0, `-log` climbs toward infinity. So confidently correct costs nothing and confidently wrong costs a lot.
- The `log` is what makes it trainable. To do well across many examples, you want the *product* of all their correct-answer probabilities to be high (probabilities of independent events multiply together). Maximizing a giant product is unwieldy, but `log` has a property that turns a product into a sum: `log(a × b) = log(a) + log(b)`. So taking the log lets you add up one loss per example instead of multiplying, which is far easier to work with. The minus sign is only there because probabilities are at most 1, so their logs come out negative, and we flip the sign to get a positive number to drive downward.

## Why `exp` and not just absolute value, or squaring?

If all you want is to turn negative numbers positive, absolute value does that. `|-5| = 5`. And if you want bigger scores to count for more, squaring does that. `(-5)² = 25`. So why reach for `exp`, which needs a special constant and a power?

The answer is that "make it positive" is too weak a description of what softmax needs. The real requirement is **positive *and* order-preserving, including across negative numbers.** A probability assignment has to keep the classifier's ranking intact: if the classifier scored cat higher than dog, then cat must come out with more probability than dog. Always.

Test the candidates against that with real numbers. Say the scores are:

```
cat  =  3     # the correct label
dog  = -5
bird =  1
```

The classifier clearly likes cat most and dog least. Watch what each function does.

**exp:**

```
exp(3) = 20.09,  exp(-5) = 0.0067,  exp(1) = 2.72     (sum = 22.82)

P(cat)  = 20.09 / 22.82 = 0.880
P(dog)  = 0.0067 / 22.82 = 0.0003
P(bird) = 2.72 / 22.82 = 0.119
```

Sensible. Cat dominates, dog is almost zero.

**absolute value:**

```
|3| = 3,  |-5| = 5,  |1| = 1     (sum = 9)

P(cat)  = 3/9 = 0.333
P(dog)  = 5/9 = 0.556
P(bird) = 1/9 = 0.111
```

**Dog now has the highest probability**, even though the classifier scored it lowest. Absolute value threw away the sign, so the strongly-rejected class (score -5) became the strongly-preferred one. Broken.

**squaring:**

```
3² = 9,  (-5)² = 25,  1² = 1     (sum = 35)

P(cat)  = 9/35  = 0.257
P(dog)  = 25/35 = 0.714
P(bird) = 1/35  = 0.029
```

Same failure, worse. Dog wins again, because squaring also ignores the sign.

Both absolute value and squaring are symmetric around zero: they treat +5 and -5 as the same. But in a classifier, +5 means "probably this class" and -5 means "probably not this class." Any function that maps those to the same output is unusable. `exp` is the natural function that always increases as its input increases, over every number including negative ones, so it never scrambles the ranking. That rules out both alternatives.

There's a second thing `exp` does that the others don't: it turns *differences* in score into *ratios* in probability. If cat scores 2 higher than dog, cat ends up about 7.4 times more probable (because `exp(2)` is about 7.4), no matter what the baseline scores are. A useful consequence is that adding the same amount to every score changes nothing, because the shift cancels out in the division. That matches the fact that only the gaps between scores carry meaning, not their absolute values.

And the deepest reason, the one that explains why it's `exp` and not something else: softmax is built on treating the score as the *logarithm* of an unnormalized probability. If you accept that framing, then to recover the probability you have to undo the logarithm, and the function that undoes `log` is `exp`. Within that framing it isn't a free choice; `exp` is the only thing that undoes the `log`. Why would a score behave like a log-probability? A linear classifier works by adding up weighted pixel values. Separately, the rule from probability is that when independent clues combine, you *multiply* their probabilities, not add them. Logarithms are the tool that connects those two: add two logarithms together and you have multiplied the original numbers. So a classifier that adds up scores is, in effect, multiplying probabilities without showing it, and `exp` and `log` are the translation between "add up scores" and "multiply probabilities."

**One last footnote: why `e`, and not 2 or 10?** For correctness, any base bigger than 1 works. `2^score` or `10^score` would give a valid ranking too, because changing the base only rescales the scores by a fixed amount that the weights can absorb during training. So the requirement is really "an exponential function." `e` (about 2.718, a fixed constant the way π is) gets picked because the rate of change of `e^x` is just `e^x` itself, with no extra multiplier, which keeps the later formulas simpler. "Raise `e` to the power" is convention; "use an exponential" is the actual necessity.

## The takeaway

Neither formula is the one right answer. Each is the written-down form of a goal:

- **Hinge loss** = "give me the widest buffer between the correct score and the wrong ones." The `max` caps the demand at good-enough; the `1` sets the buffer.
- **Softmax loss** = "make the correct label as probable as possible." The `exp` turns scores into positive, correctly-ordered probability weights; the `log` turns a product of probabilities into a sum you can add up and minimize.

People do invent other valid losses for other purposes. But once you know the goal each of these two starts from, the formulas look less arbitrary.
