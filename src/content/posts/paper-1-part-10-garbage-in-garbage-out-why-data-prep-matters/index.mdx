Neural networks are sensitive to the scale of their inputs, and pixels come in a wide range: 0 to 255.

Recall from Part 8 that a weight's update is proportional to the input feeding it: blame on a weight = incoming blame × that input's value. So a pixel of 255 pushes its weights 255 times harder than a pixel of 1. Feed raw 0-to-255 values straight into a network and the updates come out large and erratic, which makes training unstable and slow.

The fix: **normalize** the inputs, shrinking them to a small, consistent range.

## Normalization

The simplest approach: divide every pixel by 255, so the values run from 0 to 1 instead of 0 to 255.

```
normalized_pixel = raw_pixel / 255
```

Now every input is a small number in the same range, so no pixel pushes its weights wildly harder than another, and the updates stay manageable.

A more thorough approach is **standardization** (also called z-score normalization): instead of only shrinking the range, you also center it.

```
standardized_value = (raw_value - average) / standard_deviation
```

Subtract the average and divide by the standard deviation, both measured from your training data. This centers the values at zero and gives them a consistent spread. Centering helps because the network doesn't have to first learn to cancel out a constant offset before it can focus on the actual patterns. Networks generally train more easily this way.

## Why This Matters

Remember the downhill walk from Part 7: training rolls the loss downhill toward its lowest point. When inputs are on very different scales, that downhill surface gets stretched, steep in one direction and nearly flat in another. One step size can't suit both: make the step big enough to move along the flat direction and it overshoots the steep one; make it small enough for the steep direction and the flat one barely moves. Putting the inputs on a similar scale makes the surface more bowl-shaped, so each step makes even progress in every direction.

This is why normalizing inputs is a routine first step before training; the CS231n notes treat it as standard preprocessing. It's a small, cheap change that makes training more stable and usually faster.

## Beyond Pixels

Normalization isn't only for pixels. Whenever your inputs arrive on very different scales, the same problem shows up and the same fix helps:

- Financial data (prices from $1 to $10,000)
- Medical data (height in centimeters next to weight in kilograms)

Here each feature is scaled by its own average and standard deviation, so no feature dominates just because its numbers happen to be bigger. (Inputs that are already on similar scales don't need it.)

A common guideline: when your features sit on very different scales, put them on a common one before training.
