Neural networks are sensitive to the scale of their inputs, and pixels come in a wide range: 0 to 255.

Recall from Part 8 that the size of the input feeding a weight is one of the things that sets how much that weight gets adjusted: bigger inputs tend to mean bigger adjustments. Raw pixels run all the way up to 255, big enough to make those adjustments large and erratic, so training is unstable and slow.

The fix: **normalize** the inputs, shrinking them to a small, consistent range.

## Normalization

The simplest approach: divide every pixel by 255, so the values run from 0 to 1 instead of 0 to 255.

```
normalized_pixel = raw_pixel / 255
```

Now every input is between 0 and 1 instead of up to 255: small, comparable numbers for the network to work with.

A more thorough approach is **standardization**. Dividing by 255 only shrinks the numbers; standardization also re-centers them around zero.

```
standardized_value = (raw_value - average) / standard_deviation
```

Subtract the average and divide by the **standard deviation** (a measure of how spread out the values are), both measured on your training data and then applied unchanged to any new data. This puts the values around zero with a comparable spread. Inputs that are centered and evenly scaled make training easier for gradient descent, which the next section explains. Networks generally train more easily this way.

## Why This Matters

Remember the downhill walk from Part 7: training rolls the loss downhill toward its lowest point. When the inputs are large or spread across very different scales, that downhill surface gets stretched, steep in one direction and nearly flat in another. One step size can't suit both: make the step big enough to move along the flat direction and it overshoots the steep one; make it small enough for the steep direction and the flat one barely moves. Putting the inputs on a small, similar scale makes the surface more bowl-shaped, so each step makes even progress in every direction.

## Beyond Pixels

Normalization isn't only for pixels. Whenever your inputs arrive on very different scales, the same problem shows up and the same fix helps:

- Financial data (prices from $1 to $10,000)
- House data (number of bedrooms, 1 to 5, next to price in the hundreds of thousands)

Here each **feature** (each individual input measurement, like one pixel or one house's bedroom count) is standardized using its own average and standard deviation, so no single feature dominates just because its numbers happen to be bigger. (Inputs already on a small, similar scale need less of this.)

A common guideline: when your features sit on very different scales, put them on a common one before training.
