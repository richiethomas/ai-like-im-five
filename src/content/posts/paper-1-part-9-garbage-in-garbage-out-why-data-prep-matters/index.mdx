Neural networks are powerful, but they're sensitive to the scale of their inputs.

Pixel values range from 0 to 255. If you feed these directly into a network, early layers see huge values (255) and tiny values (0). Weights adjust differently for different ranges. Training becomes unstable and slow.

The solution: **normalize** your inputs.

## Normalization

The simplest approach: divide all pixel values by 255, so they range from 0 to 1 instead of 0 to 255.

```
normalized_pixel = raw_pixel / 255
```

Now all inputs are in a consistent range. Gradients flow more smoothly. Training converges faster.

A more sophisticated approach: **standardization** (z-score normalization).

```
standardized_pixel = (raw_pixel - mean) / std_dev
```

Subtract the mean of all pixels, divide by the standard deviation. This centers the data around zero with unit variance.

Why? Networks learn faster when inputs have zero mean and unit variance. Weights don't need to spend parameters learning "the overall shift"—they can focus on patterns.

## Why This Matters

Without normalization, training is like trying to walk downhill in a severely tilted landscape. You overshoot in some directions, get stuck in others. With normalization, the landscape is more balanced. Gradient descent works much more smoothly.

On CIFAR-10 (a standard benchmark), proper normalization can reduce training time by 2-3x and improve final accuracy.

## Beyond Pixels

Normalization isn't just for pixels. Any numerical input benefits from scaling:
- Financial data (stock prices range from $1 to $10,000)
- Medical data (height in cm vs. weight in kg have vastly different ranges)
- Any mixed-scale features

The rule: if your features have different scales, normalize them.
