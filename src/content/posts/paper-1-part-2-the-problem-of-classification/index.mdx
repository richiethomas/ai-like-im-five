In [the last post](/post/paper-1-part-1-what-does-it-mean-for-a-computer-to-see-a-picture/), we established the one idea this whole series stands on: to a computer, a picture is a grid of numbers. You opened the file and saw them yourself.

Now comes the task CS231n spends a semester on: **image classification**. The goal is simple to state:

Given an image (a grid of numbers), assign it a label from a fixed list of choices.

For example:

- Is this a picture of a cat or a dog?
- Is this a photograph of a car, a bike, or a truck?
- Does this X-ray show a broken bone: yes or no?

Your brain does this instantly and without effort. For a computer it is genuinely hard, hard enough that the seven challenges below are the reason simply comparing grids of numbers fails on real-world photos.

## The Seven Things That Make It Hard

CS231n, formally titled "Convolutional Neural Networks for Visual Recognition," is Stanford's introductory course on computer vision. It opens with a list of challenges. Each one is a way that two pictures of the same subject end up with wildly different numbers.

**Viewpoint variation.** A dog viewed from the front looks different from a dog viewed from the side. A dog lying down looks different from a dog standing. The dog is still a dog, but the grid of numbers changes completely depending on where the camera is. Concretely: rotate the camera 90 degrees around the dog, and nearly every value in the grid changes, even though it's the same animal.

**Scale variation.** A dog in the foreground of a photo takes up most of the image. A dog in the distance is tiny. Same dog, vastly different numbers.

**Deformation.** Dogs move. They stretch, crouch, and tilt their heads. The shape changes, so the numbers change.

**Occlusion.** Part of the dog might be hidden behind a tree or a person. Think of a dog photographed behind a picket fence, with only slivers of fur and one eye visible. The computer sees an incomplete picture but still has to produce the correct label, relying on whatever visible patterns it has learned to associate with "dog."

**Illumination conditions.** A dog in bright sunlight produces completely different numbers than the same dog in shadow or under indoor lighting. The dog's fur reflects light the same way it always did; what the camera records is the combination of that fur and the light hitting it.

**Background clutter.** If the dog is sitting in a park, the background is a mess of trees and grass. The computer has to separate the numbers that belong to "dog" from the numbers that belong to everything else. That's surprisingly hard.

**Intra-class variation.** A Chihuahua and a Great Dane are both dogs, but they look almost nothing alike. Think of a Poodle, a Bulldog, and a Dalmatian: three dogs that share almost no obvious visual features, yet all belong to the same class. The computer needs to find the common thread underneath huge visual variation.

## Why This Matters

Much of image classification, in some sense, comes down to clever solutions to these seven problems.

A naive approach, comparing the number grids directly, fails in general for exactly the seven reasons above, though it can still work in narrow, controlled cases like matching a fixed logo photographed under identical lighting and angle every time. If you have a reference picture of a dog and you go looking for other pictures with identical numbers, you'll fail constantly: viewpoint, lighting, occlusion, and a hundred other things will have changed the grid.

We need methods that are robust to these variations, meaning the answer stays right even when the picture changes. Methods that say "yes, this is a dog" even when the dog is rotated, occluded, far away, or in shadow.

This is what CS231n teaches us to build, one idea per post: first nearest neighbor (guess by finding the most similar stored example), then linear classifiers (score each possible label with a simple weighted recipe), then the convolutional networks that tackle all seven challenges.

![The same dog, moved down and to the right](/experiments/classification/dog-moved.png)

![The same dog, behind a fence](/experiments/classification/dog-fence.png)

Now open their number files side by side with the original:

- [dog-moved.ppm.txt](/experiments/classification/dog-moved.ppm.txt): the dog only shifted position, yet **more than half the numbers in the file changed**. Every pixel the dog moved onto, and every pixel it moved off of, has new values.
- [dog-fence.ppm.txt](/experiments/classification/dog-fence.ppm.txt): scan for the stripes of `140 140 140`. Those gray columns replaced whole stretches of dog numbers.

To you, all three pictures are obviously the same dog. To a program comparing numbers, they barely agree. That gap between "obviously the same" and "numerically different" is the entire problem of computer vision, and the seven challenges above are the ways that gap opens up.
