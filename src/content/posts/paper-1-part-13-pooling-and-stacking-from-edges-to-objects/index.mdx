After a convolution layer, feature maps are the same size as the input (or slightly smaller). If the input is 224×224, the output might be 222×222.

Apply another convolution, get 220×220. Keep going and the maps stay large. But computation explodes and patterns are still local.

**Pooling** solves this. It downsamples: takes a region (like 2×2) and outputs a single value. Common pooling: **max pooling**. Take the maximum value in each 2×2 region.

```
Input:           Max Pooling (2×2):
1 2 3 4          2 4
5 6 7 8    →     6 8
9 10 11 12
13 14 15 16
```

Output is half the size in each dimension. A 224×224 map becomes 112×112.

## Why Pooling Works

**Aggregation:** Pooling combines information from a small region into one value. Exact positions don't matter; only whether a feature is present.

**Robustness:** If a pattern shifts by 1-2 pixels, max pooling ignores it. This robustness is valuable for recognition.

**Dimensionality reduction:** Smaller feature maps = faster computation.

## The Hierarchy

Stacking convolution and pooling layers builds a hierarchy:

```
Input image (224×224)
  ↓ Conv (detect edges)
  ↓ Pool (224→112)
  ↓ Conv (detect edge combinations, i.e., textures)
  ↓ Pool (112→56)
  ↓ Conv (detect texture combinations, i.e., shapes)
  ↓ Pool (56→28)
  ↓ Conv (detect complex shapes, i.e., object parts)
  ↓ Pool (28→14)
  ↓ Fully connected layers (make the final decision)
```

Early layers detect simple features. Later layers detect complex combinations. By the time you reach a fully-connected layer, you're working with high-level semantic concepts, not raw pixels.

A cat classifier might have learned: "If I see these edge patterns here, and those texture combinations there, it's a cat."
