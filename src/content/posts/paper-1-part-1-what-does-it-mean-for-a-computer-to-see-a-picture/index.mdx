We're going to spend this series working through [CS231n](https://cs231n.github.io/), the course number of Stanford's free class on how computers learn to see. It's a foundational course in deep learning, the kind of course that builds the intuition you need before tackling dense research papers. It also sits at the start of a famous reading list that [Ilya Sutskever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Sutskever) (a co-founder of OpenAI and one of the researchers behind ChatGPT) is said to have given [John Carmack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carmack) (the programmer behind the video games Doom and Quake).

## What We're Building Is a Function

Here's the goal, in one sentence: everything in this series is aimed at building a **function**.

You met functions in high school. `y = mx + b` takes a number `x`, does some arithmetic, and hands back a number `y`: give it an input, get an output.

What we're going to build is the same idea, just bigger. The input, x, is a picture. The output, y, is a guess about what the picture shows, like "dog". We find this function by showing it many pictures whose answers we already know and adjusting it until its guesses are usually right, so that it then works on new pictures it has never seen. The finished function is called a **model**.

That is all a model is: a picture goes in, a guess comes out, and in between is a function we built from examples. Everything else in this series (turning input pictures into numbers the function can work with, scoring them, measuring the mistakes, improving the guesses, and turning the output numbers back into an answer a person can understand) is detail in service of building that one function.

To see what "adjusting it until its guesses are right" looks like, picture each example as a dot on a graph, blue for cats and orange for dogs, placed so that similar pictures sit near each other. The function is a line: it guesses "cat" on one side and "dog" on the other. A dot on the correct side is a right guess; a dot on the wrong side is a mistake.

That line is just `y = mx + b` again. Training adjusts its two knobs, the slope `m` and the intercept `b` (also called the **bias**), to get more dots on the correct side.

Nudging those two knobs until more dots end up on the correct side is the whole job of training. Sorting labeled dots into their groups like this even has a name, **classification**, and it is what the next several posts are about.

But before we build anything, we need to ask the most basic question: what does a computer actually receive when we hand it a picture?

## A Picture Is Just Numbers

Your brain sees a photo and almost instantly knows it contains a dog. You don't think about it. The image "makes sense" to you.

A computer doesn't work that way. A computer can only work with numbers. (Underneath, even those numbers are stored as tiny on-off switches, but thinking of them as numbers is enough for our purposes.) So before a computer can "understand" an image at all, the image has to be converted into numbers. By "understand" we just mean: map the numbers to the correct label. The computer isn't doing anything like human comprehension.

Every image on your screen is stored as a grid of [pixels](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel), tiny colored squares. If the image is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels tall, that's an 800 × 600 grid. Each pixel's color is written down as three numbers: how much red, how much green, and how much blue it contains (the [RGB](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGB) system). Each of those three numbers is a whole number from 0 (none of that color) to 255 (as much as possible).

So an 800 × 600 image is really an 800 × 600 × 3 grid of numbers. That's **1.44 million numbers** to represent one photo. (The 0 to 255 range is the most common convention. Some formats allow a wider range. And neural networks (programs that learn from examples, which we'll meet later in this series) often shrink these values down to sit between 0 and 1, because that helps them learn more reliably.)

When you scroll through your photos, your phone is shuffling around millions of numbers. And when you compress an image for email (that is, when you shrink its file size so it uploads faster and takes up less storage), you're playing one of two tricks with those numbers:

- **Write the repeats as shorthand.** A clear blue sky means the same three numbers repeated thousands of times in a row. A format can store that as, in effect, "sky color, five thousand times" instead of writing it out five thousand times. Reversing the shorthand gives back every original number exactly. This is a simplified version of what is called "lossless compression": real formats like PNG use a more elaborate method (called DEFLATE), but the idea of storing repeats compactly is the same.
- **Throw away detail your eye won't miss.** A format can also smooth over tiny differences between neighboring pixels and store the simpler result. The numbers that come back are close to the originals, but not identical. This is called lossy compression, it's what JPEG files do, and it's why JPEGs are so small.

Either way, compressing a picture is just doing arithmetic on a grid of numbers.

This is where computers start. Not with "a picture of a dog." With a giant grid of numbers.

## Try It Yourself

Don't take my word for it. Here is a 64 × 64 pixel cartoon dog:

![A 64 by 64 pixel cartoon dog](/experiments/picture-is-numbers/dog.png)

This little dog is a 64 × 64 grid: about twelve thousand numbers. That's enough for a blocky cartoon but nowhere near enough for fur, whiskers, or shadows, which is why it looks like a drawing rather than a real dog. A photo from your phone is the exact same kind of thing, just bigger. A 12-megapixel photo is a grid of about 36 million numbers, roughly three thousand times more than our dog. More numbers means finer detail, but the structure never changes.

And here is **the exact same picture, as a file of numbers you can read**: [dog.ppm.txt](/experiments/picture-is-numbers/dog.ppm.txt).

That file is a real image format (called "plain PPM") that image editors such as the free program [GIMP](https://www.gimp.org/) will open as a picture. But because it stores its numbers as ordinary text, you can also just read it, in your browser or in any text editor. No special software needed.

Things to look for when you open it:

- After the short header, every group of three numbers is one pixel: red, green, blue.
- The file starts with `170 210 240` repeated over and over. That's the sky: some red, more green, a lot of blue makes light blue. (It's also exactly the kind of repetition that the lossless shorthand trick from earlier collapses.)
- Scroll down until the numbers change to `100 60 30`. More red than green, very little blue: that's the dark brown of the dog's ears.
- Keep scrolling and you'll find `25 25 25` (the nearly-black eyes and nose) and `230 120 140` (the pink tongue).

The dog you see and the wall of numbers are the same thing. That single idea powers everything in this series.
